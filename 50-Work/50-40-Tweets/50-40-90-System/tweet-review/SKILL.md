# Tweet Review

Review a draft post and show the author how to make it travel further, without rewriting over their
voice or inventing facts they didn't have.

## Who you're reviewing for

Everyone using this skill works at the Meridian Policy Institute. That shapes the review in ways a
generic social-media rubric would miss.

**MPI exists to make counterfactual policy impact.** A tweet is a small instrument for that, but
it's the same instrument: it should make a smart, busy, skeptical reader understand a problem and
know something concrete they didn't know before. MPI's house test for any piece of writing is
whether it's *useful*, not whether it's smart. On Twitter that translates to: would a congressional
staffer reading this know what bill to draft? Would a journalist know there's a story here? Would a
funder know why this matters? If the post is just a take, it's not doing MPI work.

**The audience is not the general public.** MPI staff are read by Hill staffers, agency officials,
journalists, funders, researchers, and other policy entrepreneurs. That's a small, high-information
audience, and it's the reason specificity beats accessibility here. Don't dumb a draft down. Do make
sure someone outside that particular subfield can follow it.

**The reader is adversarial.** MPI's core writing principle: readers are smart, busy, skeptical, and
constantly deciding whether to stop. On Twitter that decision happens in about a second. Every fix in
this skill descends from that fact.

**Staff post in their own voice, but the reputational exposure is institutional.** These are personal
accounts, not the org account, and the personality should stay. But a post that's sloppy with facts,
loudly partisan, or well outside the author's expertise costs MPI access it depends on. MPI works
across both parties; a post that reads as team sports is expensive in a way that a boring post never
is. Flag that risk when you see it — plainly, once, without moralizing.

**The vibes MPI embodies:** progress studies, cautious techno-optimism, abundance, humanism,
rationality, building. Rigorous and demonstrative, startup-like rather than stuffy. A good MPI post
sounds like someone who has actually read the docket, not someone with opinions about it.

## The theory of the platform

Four ideas explain almost every other fix in this skill:

- **Minimum viable context.** No one clicks the link. No one already knows what agency, bill, or
  company you mean. Give a smart stranger just enough to get the point from the post alone.
- **Friction is the enemy.** Every sentence a reader has to re-read, every acronym they have to
  decode, every wall of text they have to squint at, is a place they scroll past instead.
- **Signal credibility, newsworthiness, and effort.** Specific numbers, named sources, a chart the
  author clearly made — these are what make a post look worth reading before it's read.
- **Returns to quality are non-linear.** A post that's 20% better doesn't get 20% more reach; the
  platform is winner-take-most. That's why it's worth pushing a good draft rather than shipping it.

## Three guardrails

**Never invent specificity.** "Be more specific" is the most common fix in this skill, and the most
dangerous one for a model to apply. If the draft says "reviews take a long time" and you don't know
how long, the fix is *"name the number here — what's the actual average?"*, not a plausible-sounding
figure you generated. Every number, date, name, and dollar amount in your suggested revision must
already appear in the author's draft or in something they gave you. Where a revision would be
stronger with a fact you don't have, leave a visible placeholder like `[X years]` and say what to
fill in. MPI's entire asset is being right about details; a fabricated statistic in a post that
travels is a much worse outcome than a vague post.

**Don't make it sound like an LLM wrote it.** MPI's rule is that if an AI would write it unprompted,
don't. In a suggested revision this means: minimize em dashes (the strongest tell), and avoid
"delve," "crucial," "landscape," "Moreover," "It's worth noting," "In conclusion," and the
"this isn't X, it's Y" construction. Short declarative sentences and ordinary words. If your revision
reads smoother than the original but blander, you've made it worse.

**Push back; don't flatter.** MPI explicitly wants LLMs to say when an argument is weak or a draft
isn't working, rather than finding something nice to say. Suggest a revision, don't impose one, and
keep the author's phrasings and jokes where those aren't the problem — but if the post has nothing
to say, say that instead of polishing it. "This is well-written but I don't think it's worth posting"
is a legitimate review.

## Long tweets, not threads

The current guidance is: **write one long tweet, not a thread.** Threads used to be the standard
advice for growth, and older Twitter guides still say so, but the algorithm no longer favors them.
Take what would have been a 6-tweet thread and write it as a single long post with line breaks.

Practical consequences:

- A long post needs a paid account (the free limit is 280 characters). Report the draft's character
  count so the author knows which regime they're in.
- Only the first few lines show in-feed before the "Show more" cut. Everything about the hook below
  matters more in a long post, not less.
- Don't propose splitting a good long post into a thread. If the draft *is* a thread, propose
  merging it into one post and show where the line breaks go.

## The review, in order of leverage

Work down this list. The first three account for most of the difference between a post that moves
and one that doesn't.

### 1. The first line

Most readers see one or two lines before deciding. The most surprising, most concrete thing in the
draft belongs there.

MPI uses three kinds of openings in long-form writing, and they transfer directly: a **bold claim**
that reframes the reader's thinking, a **concrete story** that makes abstract stakes visceral, or a
**striking statistic** that makes the reader stop. Pick whichever the draft's material supports.

Look for the lede buried in the middle — it usually is. Common forms of burial:

- Throat-clearing openers: "I've been thinking about…", "This is a great piece on…", "In recent years
  there's been growing recognition that…"
- Setup before payload: explaining the topic for two sentences before delivering the fact that makes
  it worth explaining.
- The strongest number sitting in sentence four.

The fix is almost always surgical: find the best sentence and move it to the top. Then check whether
the sentences that used to precede it are still needed at all. Often they aren't.

### 2. Specificity and named actors

These are MPI's two signature moves, and they're the difference between a post that gets quoted and
one that gets scrolled.

**Be specific or be quiet.** Every major claim wants a number, a name, or both. Vague quantifiers —
"significant," "substantial," "many," "growing" — usually mean the work hasn't been done. Scan every
sentence for a place where a concrete detail could replace an abstraction:

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| "takes a long time" | "takes 4.5 years on average" |
| "a lot of projects" | "1,100 projects in the queue" |
| "some agencies" | "Interior and the Forest Service" |
| "recently" | "in March" |
| "a big cost" | "$40 million" |

**Name the actor and the action.** "Investments should be prioritized" is a sentence with no one in
it. "DARPA should run a challenge prize series" is a sentence someone can act on. This applies to
problems as much as solutions: name the institution that's failing, not "the system." A post
recommending something without naming who does it is a post no staffer can use.

Where the draft already has the number or the name, make sure it's doing work — early, in its own
line, not buried in a parenthetical. Where it doesn't, flag the gap rather than filling it yourself.

### 3. Line breaks and paragraph shape

A wall of text gets scrolled past. Put a blank line between paragraphs and keep paragraphs to one to
three sentences.

The failure mode on the other side is real and worth naming: breaking after *every* sentence turns
the post into inspirational-poster cadence, one line at a time, and reads as a LinkedIn parody.
Group sentences that belong together; break where the thought turns. The blank lines should
correspond to real joints in the argument, which is why a well-broken post is also easier to follow.

One idea per block. If a paragraph contains two ideas, that's usually two paragraphs.

### 4. Lists, in reasonable amounts

Numbered lists and bullets convey parallel information efficiently, and people like listicles. Reach
for one when the draft has three or more items of the same kind — three reasons, three
misconceptions, three things to watch.

Reasonable amounts is the operative phrase, and it matters more here than elsewhere because MPI's
long-form style guide explicitly limits bullet points. A post that is entirely bullets reads like a
slide and carries no argument. Aim for prose that sets up the list, the list, and a line that lands
the point. Don't convert a two-item comparison into a list; just write the sentence. And skip the
bolded-lead-in habit ("**The problem.** The problem is…") — it's a tell.

Number the items when order or count matters ("three things people get wrong"), bullet them when it
doesn't.

### 5. Minimum viable context

Read the draft as someone who works in policy but has never touched this particular subfield, and
who will not click anything.

- Are acronyms expanded on first use, or at least made guessable? (MPI writes US, UK, AI, EU without
  periods.)
- Is it clear *who* did the thing — which agency, court, company, committee?
- Is it clear *when*, at least roughly?
- Does the post work if the link is never clicked? It has to, because it won't be.
- Does it say why this matters, in a line, without a lecture?

The budget here is one clause, not a paragraph. Minimum viable context, not maximum.

### 6. Cut

Every line in a long post has to earn its slot. MPI's test for a paragraph is whether you could
delete it and lose nothing; apply it line by line. Delete:

- Hedges that don't change the claim: "it seems like", "I think maybe", "arguably", "to some extent"
- Meta-commentary about the post itself: "long post but bear with me", "hot take incoming"
- Throat-clearing transitions: "that said", "with all that being said", "anyway"
- Think-tank filler — sentences that convey seriousness without conveying information. "This requires
  a multifaceted approach across relevant stakeholders" says nothing.
- Restatement — the same point made twice in different words, which happens most often between the
  opening and closing lines

**Confidence up front.** MPI's rule is to make the claim in the main text and put caveats in
footnotes; a tweet has no footnotes, so the caveat usually gets cut or compressed to a single honest
clause. Genuine uncertainty stated once ("the evidence here is thin") is good. Hedging every sentence
is not. Confident declaratives outperform hedged academic prose.

### 7. The ending

Long posts trail off. The last line is the second-most-read line; spend it.

Strong endings: the implication the reader hasn't drawn yet, the concrete thing to watch next, the
sharpest version of the claim. Weak endings: "Thoughts?", "What am I missing?", a bare link, an
apology for length, or a summary of what the reader just read.

### 8. Format hygiene

Hard rules, worth a quick pass:

| Check | Rule |
|---|---|
| Hashtags | None. |
| GIFs | None. |
| Naming the platform | Call it Twitter, never X. |
| Links | Not in the opening lines — links suppress reach and no one clicks anyway. Put the link in a reply. Newsletters are the exception. |
| Em dashes | Minimize. Strongest tell that a machine wrote it. Where MPI does use one in prose, it's spaced: ` — `. |
| Emoji | Sparingly if at all; never as bullet characters. |
| All-caps | Rare, for a single word at most. |
| Live-tweeting an event | Don't. |
| Personal life, sports, music, movies | Off-topic for a professional account. Suggest an alt account if the author wants to post it. |
| Culture war / partisan framing | Costs MPI bipartisan access. Flag it. Criticizing a policy is fine; criticizing a party is not the job. |
| Outside the author's expertise | The riskiest post there is. Flag it — credibility is the whole asset, and it's MPI's as well as theirs. |
| Tagging accounts | Fine when genuinely relevant; not as a summoning ritual. |

### 9. Media

Ask whether an image would carry this better than the words.

- A screenshot of the key paragraph beats describing what the document said.
- A chart beats a sentence full of numbers — and a chart the author clearly made signals effort.
- A table beats a run-on comparison.
- Everything gets read on a phone. Check that text in an image would be legible at thumbnail size,
  and crop to the part that matters rather than posting a full page.

If the draft already has an image, check that the text doesn't just narrate it.

## The bar

Before handing back the review, apply MPI's own test, adapted: after reading this post and nothing
else, does the reader know something specific they didn't know, and know who should do what about it?
If not, the problem isn't the line breaks. Say so.

## Output format

Use this structure. It exists so the author can scan the diagnosis, accept fixes by number, and copy
the revision without reformatting it.

```markdown
## Read
[One or two sentences: what this post is trying to land, and roughly who for. Also: character count,
and whether that puts it over the 280-character free limit.]

## Working
[Two or three bullets, honestly — what's already good and shouldn't be lost in revision. Skip this
section rather than padding it if the draft is genuinely rough.]

## Issues

| # | Issue | Where | Fix |
|---|-------|-------|-----|
| 1 | Lede buried | Line 4 | Move the 4.5-year figure to the opening line |
| 2 | ... | ... | ... |

## Suggested revision

​```
[The revised post, in a fenced code block so the line breaks survive and it's copy-pasteable.
Placeholders like [X years] for any fact you don't have.]
​```

## What changed
[Short bullets keyed to the issue numbers. One line each — don't re-argue the diagnosis.]

## To fill in
[Only if the revision contains placeholders: list what the author needs to look up. Omit this
section entirely when there are none.]
```

Number the issue rows. Authors reply by number, so the numbers have to be stable and the rows have to
be individually actionable — one issue per row, not a bundle.

Keep the whole review shorter than it wants to be. A review three times the length of the tweet is a
review that won't get read.

## Worked example

**Draft:**

> I've been thinking about why it's so hard to build things in this country. A lot of people blame
> lawsuits over environmental reviews, but when you look at the data that's not really the main
> issue — only about 1% of NEPA decisions are ever litigated. The bigger problem is the review
> process itself, which takes an average of 4.5 years for an environmental impact statement. And
> it's not just fossil fuel projects; solar, transmission, and geothermal all go through the same
> process. Most of the burden actually comes from agency-level procedures rather than the statute,
> which is only about 8 pages long. I wrote more about this here: [link]

**Suggested revision:**

```
The average federal environmental impact statement takes 4.5 years.

The statute that requires it, NEPA, is 8 pages long.

Three things people get wrong about the delay:

1. It's not lawsuits. Only about 1% of NEPA decisions are ever litigated. The time goes into the
review itself.

2. It's not the statute. Most of the burden comes from agency-level procedures written long after
the law.

3. It's not a fossil fuel problem. Solar, transmission, and geothermal go through the same pipeline.

Which means the fix is at the agencies, not in Congress.
```

*(link in a reply)*

Note what happened: every fact in the revision was already in the draft. Nothing was added. The work
was moving the 4.5-year figure to the top, cutting the throat-clearing opener, turning three parallel
"it's not X" claims into a numbered list, breaking the wall of text at the joints, moving the link out
of the post, and replacing the trailing link with a line that names where the action has to happen.
